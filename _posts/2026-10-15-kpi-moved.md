A conversion rate jumps 18% in one month. Leadership celebrates. The growth team gets congratulated. The dashboard gets screenshotted and put in the board deck.

Then someone mentions that the team changed the definition of "lead" on the 12th of the month. The denominator shrank. The business didn't change at all.

I've seen this happen more than once. It's rarely malicious. It's a measurement change that nobody thought to flag, and the celebration was entirely based on a number that stopped measuring what everyone thought it was measuring.

## 1. Where the 18% Came From

A KPI is a function of three things: the underlying business reality, the definition of the metric, and the data pipeline that produces the number. When the KPI moves, everyone assumes the business moved. But any of the three can move it.

When the definition or the pipeline changes without anyone noting it, you get a step change that has nothing to do with performance. The chart above shows the shape: a flat underlying business with a sudden 18% jump the month the definition changed.

## 2. How Silent Drift Happens

Definition changes are normal. Teams refine metrics all the time. The problems aren't the changes themselves. The problems are:

- The change isn't versioned. The definition lives in a Slack thread, a LookML file, a Tableau calculation, or someone's head. When it changes, the time series keeps the same name and the same dashboard tile.
- The change isn't dated. There's no marker on the chart saying "definition changed here."
- The change happens mid-period, so part of the month is on the old definition and part is on the new one. The monthly number is a mix of two different metrics.

Common silent drivers, roughly in the order I see them:

1. **Denominator change.** "Active user" used to mean "logged in this month." Now it means "logged in or opened an email." Same metric name, different denominator, sudden jump.
2. **Filter change.** A report used to exclude internal test accounts. Someone removes the filter to "simplify the query." Volume goes up.
3. **Source system change.** The CRM swaps to a new backend. The same field means something slightly different in the new system.
4. **Deduplication logic change.** The pipeline used to dedupe by `email`. Now it dedupes by `customer_id`. Different row count.
5. **Scope change.** A region, a product line, or a customer segment gets added or removed from the population.

Any of these, applied without a flag, produces a KPI move that looks like a business move.

## 3. Three Habits That Catch It

**Version every metric.** Each definition should have a version, an owner, a start date, and an end date. The dashboard tile should show the version. When the version changes, split the time series. Don't continue it.

**Mark definition changes on the chart.** A vertical line, a shaded region, an annotation. Enough for anyone reading the chart to know the step isn't performance.

**Investigate step changes before celebrating them.** A KPI that jumps 10% or more in one period is more likely a measurement change than a business change. Real business changes are gradual. The first question when a step appears is "did the definition or the pipeline change?", not "what did we do right?"

## 4. What This Changes in Practice

- A KPI time series is only meaningful if the definition is constant along its length. The same name does not guarantee the same definition.
- The faster a KPI moves, the more likely the move is measurement, not business. Trends build; steps reflect a change in how the number is produced.
- Every metric in a leadership dashboard should carry a definition, an owner, and a version. A metric without those three is a number, not a metric.
- The person who changed the definition is rarely in the meeting where the KPI is celebrated. Bridging that gap is a BI responsibility, not a leadership responsibility.

The part that annoys me is that this is so easy to prevent. A version number. A line on a chart. A question asked before the celebration instead of after. None of it is hard. It just doesn't feel as good as a big green arrow on the dashboard.
